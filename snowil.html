Remote sensing class case study · EAE 560 · Spring 2025

Is Illinois losing its spring snow cover?

Snow cover is a familiar part of winter in Illinois, but how much snow stays on the ground can vary tremendously from one year to the next. Does that variability hide any longer term changes? And might those changes look different in the middle of winter than they do as spring approaches?

For my EAE 560 Remote Sensing of the Environment term project, I used Landsat-8 satellite imagery to examine snow coverage across Illinois during January and March from 2014 through 2025. I was interested in whether the two months showed similar patterns and how much snow coverage varied from year to year.

The bigger picture

Why study snow cover?

Snow on the ground affects much more than how a winter landscape looks. Snow cover influences the amount of sunlight reflected by the surface, the timing of snowmelt and runoff, soil conditions, ecosystems, transportation, agriculture, and winter recreation.

Snow is very reflective, so it helps keep the surface cool by sending a substantial portion of incoming sunlight back toward space. When snow disappears earlier in the season, darker land surfaces absorb more solar energy.

Studies across North America have documented changes in snow depth, snow mass, and spring snow coverage, although the trends differ across regions and seasons. The Great Lakes region is especially interesting because temperatures, precipitation, and lake effects can influence winter conditions in different ways.

What I wanted to find out:

  • How did snow coverage in Illinois vary between 2014 and 2025?
  • Did January and March show similar or different patterns?
  • How variable was the amount of snow from year to year?
  • Were the patterns consistent with findings from larger North American studies?

The approach

How can we measure snow cover from space?

I used Landsat-8 imagery accessed through Google Earth Engine, with analysis performed in Google Colab. For each year from 2014 through 2025, I created a mosaic of available January images and another for March, clipped to the Illinois state boundary.

I used the Landsat QA Pixel band to mask clouds, then calculated the Normalized Difference Snow Index (NDSI). This index takes advantage of the different ways snow reflects visible green and shortwave infrared light.

NDSI = (Green − SWIR) / (Green + SWIR)

For Landsat-8, I used Band 3 (green) and Band 6 (shortwave infrared). Pixels with NDSI greater than 0.4 were classified as snow. I then calculated the area identified as snow in each monthly mosaic and plotted the annual values for January and March.

I also explored the possibility of using ArcGIS Pro to identify spatial snow cover hotspots, but a problem with the time formatting of the multidimensional raster data prevented that part of the analysis from being completed.

These are estimates from available cloud-masked Landsat observations, not continuous measurements of snow cover throughout each month. Cloud cover, satellite sampling, and the way images are combined can affect the amount of snow identified.

The findings

What happened in January and March?

The biggest result was how different the two months looked. In this 12-year sample, the fitted January snow area trend was positive, while the fitted March trend was negative Both months had considerable year to year variability.

12 years January and March observations from 2014 through 2025.

January: More variation, with some especially snowy years

January showed large differences between years. In the satellite sample, 2021, 2022, and 2025 were among the years with unusually high classified snow area, while 2017, 2020, and 2023 had very little identified snow.

March: A different pattern

March showed a downward fitted trend in classified snow area. Some of the largest March values occurred earlier in the record, particularly in 2014 and 2017, while several later years had relatively little identified snow.

What did the satellite actually see?

The NDSI maps show where the satellite identified snow in the available imagery. Across the sample, snow was often more concentrated in northern Illinois, though the geographic pattern varied between years. January 2025 showed an unusual concentration farther into central Illinois.

Making sense of the results

Does this mean Illinois is losing its spring snow cover?

Not necessarily. The downward March trend is interesting, because it resembles patterns reported in broader North American studies. But twelve years is a short period for evaluating climate trends, and the large differences between individual years make interpretation difficult. The limitations of satellite sampling also impact these results. Landsat imagery cannot provide a continuous view through clouds, and a collection of images from one month does not tell us exactly how many days snow remained on the ground.

An important distinction:

Snow cover describes the area of land covered by snow. Snowfall describes how much snow falls. Snow depth measures how deep the snow is, and snow water equivalent describes how much water it contains. These measurements are related, but they are not interchangeable. This project examined snow coverage area, not snowfall totals, snow depth, or how long an uninterrupted snowpack persisted.

The results provide an interesting starting point, but a longer record and additional observations are needed to establish whether the regional patterns represent lasting climate changes.

Why it matters

Why should we care how long snow stays on the ground?

Changes in snow coverage can affect both natural systems and everyday activities. Snow insulates the soil, influences the timing of runoff and soil moisture, and provides habitat conditions for some plants and animals.

Snow conditions also matter for transportation, road maintenance, agriculture, outdoor work, tourism, and winter recreation. A region with occasional heavy snowstorms but little persistent snow cover can experience winter very differently from one where snow remains on the ground for weeks.

What would be useful to know next?

Beyond measuring snow coverage on individual satellite observations, I'd like to better understand how continuous snow cover is changing: how often snow remains on the ground, how long it lasts, and how these patterns differ around the Great Lakes.

Those questions could help connect changes in winter climate with ecological conditions, water resources, and activities that depend on sustained snow cover.

Looking ahead

Where does this work go from here?

A longer analysis would help separate year to year variability from changes occurring over decades. Additional satellite products and historical observations could also provide more complete spatial and temporal coverage.

Coming soon: Great Lakes snow cover.

I'm interested in expanding this work beyond Illinois to examine regional changes in snow cover and its persistence across the Great Lakes.
