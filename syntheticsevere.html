How might severe thunderstorms change as the climate warms?

Severe thunderstorms bring damaging winds, hail, and tornadoes to communities across the United States. We know the environments that support these storms are changing, but what might that mean for when and where storms happen?

Our study used high resolution climate simulations to examine how severe convective storm activity might evolve by the middle and end of this century. We looked at changes in frequency, the time of year storms occur, where activity is concentrated, and the most active storm days.

Published paper: Roufa et al. (2026), “Projected Mid- and Late-Century Changes in Severe Convective Storms Across the United States From Dynamically Downscaled Climate Simulations,” International Journal of Climatology.

The study

How do we study storms that haven't happened yet?

We used 3.75-km convection-permitting WRF climate simulations, which are capable of resolving thunderstorms. The simulations covered historical (1990–2005), mid-century (2040–2055), and late-century (2085–2100) periods, with two future greenhouse gas emissions pathways.

We do not have output variables that designate a severe storm, tornado, damaging wind, or severe hail. So, we identified synthetic severe storm proxies using simulated strong upward motion (upward vertical velocity) and radar reflectivity, checking this method against historical U.S. storm reports before applying it to the future simulations.

The geography of storms

Where might storm activity change?

Across the simulations, severe storm activity occurs more frequently east of its traditional regions, with more severe convective storm days in parts of the Southeast, Midwest, and Northeast. Some areas of the Great Plains see decreases during summer. These regional differences matter, because an eastward shift could bring more storm activity into places with more people and infrastructure.

A changing storm season

Will the severe storm season change?

The simulations suggest that activity could become more common earlier in the year, particularly during spring and early summer. June remains the peak month across the scenarios, but the severe storm season may start earlier and extend into the cooler months. The changes are not uniform everywhere nor in every month.

The busiest storm days

What about especially active severe weather days?

It's not just the average day that changes. In the highest emissions end-century simulation, days exceeding the historical 90th-percentile threshold of synthetic storm detections become about 82% more frequent. The chance of exceeding that historical threshold increases from 10% to 18%.

We used a threshold based on the upper end of the daily synthetic detection distribution. That is a measure of widespread modeled severe-storm activity, not a count of officially identified tornado or severe-storm outbreaks.

The atmosphere behind the changes

What might explain these patterns?

Warmer air can hold more moisture, contributing to greater atmospheric instability, which is the energy available for thunderstorm updrafts. Our simulations show increases in instability, but also changes in convective inhibition, which can keep storms from forming, and wind shear, which helps storms organize. How those ingredients come together changes with the season and region.

Why does this matter? Severe weather risk depends on more than the atmosphere. Where people live, where infrastructure is built, and how communities prepare also influence the impacts of a storm. These projections can help inform conversations about long-term planning and resilience, but aren't predictions of damage or of individual hazards.
